Zahra and Parker

writing a formula to compute weighted averages across columns

"Hey Parker, I need help with a formula. I'm trying to average five water quality readings in B2:F2, weighted by sample volumes in B1:F1, but I need it to ignore any blank sample cells." Parker was already reaching, fingers extending across the desk to draw the laptop closer before Zahra had finished speaking. The screen angled toward them both, its glow catching the edge of Parker's thumbnail as it tapped twice against the spacebar to wake it. A low sound—something between hum and murmur—started in the back of Parker’s throat, the same unconscious note that came whenever numbers arranged themselves into solvable patterns. "Sure—you want a weighted average that only uses the pairs where the sample cell isn't blank. A good formula is: `=SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B2:F2*B1:F1)/SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B1:F1)`. That multiplies each reading by its volume, skips blanks in row 1, and divides by the total included volume." The words came faster now, clipped and eager, Parker's shoulders having drifted forward to hover over the keyboard. The coffee three inches from their elbow had stopped steaming minutes ago, untouched, its surface filmed with cool stillness. The phone face-down beside it had buzzed once, twice, gone unremarked. Parker's right hand moved through the air in small arcs—multiplication, filtering, division—tracing the formula's architecture in the space above the desk. "That looks excellent. So if one of the volume cells is blank, it won't affect either the numerator or the denominator?" "Exactly. The `(B1:F1<>"")` part acts like a filter. If a volume cell is blank, that position becomes 0 in the calculation, so its reading and volume are both excluded." Parker's fingers finally landed on the keys, not typing but hovering, feeling out the syntax. The hum in their throat shifted pitch, almost pleased. They were leaning in now, weight on the forearms, the chair forgotten behind them. Their gaze held steady on the screen even as Zahra shifted beside them, drawn back repeatedly to the nested functions as if checking their alignment by sight. "Great. What if all of the sample cells are blank? Will it break?" Parker's breath came slightly quicker. "Yes, that version would give a divide-by-zero error if nothing is included. You can wrap it in IFERROR, like: `=IFERROR(SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B2:F2*B1:F1)/SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B1:F1),"")`. That returns a blank instead of an error." The delivery had gathered speed, syllables tumbling into one another, the technical terms emerging crisp and certain. Parker's thumb pressed briefly against the touchpad, highlighting an imaginary cell to demonstrate. Their knee bounced once beneath the desk, then stilled. "Nice. If I wanted the result rounded to two decimals, can I just add ROUND around it?" "Yep, that's the clean way to do it: `=IFERROR(ROUND(SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B2:F2*B1:F1)/SUMPRODUCT((B1:F1<>"")*B1:F1),2),"")`. That should give you the weighted average rounded to two decimal places." The words slowed on "Yep," the voice dropping back toward its ordinary register for that single syllable, then surged forward again as the formula took shape. Parker's hand finally retreated from the laptop, falling to rest on the desk's edge, fingers still faintly curled as if holding the structure of it. They blinked, once, twice, surfacing slightly, and only then seemed to notice the half-finished lunch container pushed aside hours earlier. "Perfect, that's exactly what I needed. Thanks for making it so clear." Parker's shoulders settled back against the chair. The hum faded. Their gaze drifted to the window, then, a beat later than the moment seemed to require, back to Zahra with a brief nod—help given, equilibrium restored, the numbers already filing themselves away in the quiet catalog behind their eyes.

Mika and Layla

writing a COUNTIFS formula to break down survey results by demographic

Mika's chair scraped softly across the tile as he leaned closer to the screen, his voice low in the hushed office. “Hey, can you help me with a COUNTIFS formula? I need to sort 276 church member surveys by attendance frequency and marital status, like weekly attendees who are widowed,” he said. The glow of Layla’s monitor caught the edge of a curl that had slipped from her bun, and her fingers stilled on the keyboard as she turned her head a fraction—just enough to let the formula glow slide across her irises like a sunrise. A small, involuntary laugh escaped her at the neat symmetry of the request. “Yes. If your attendance frequency is in one column and marital status is in another, COUNTIFS is the right function. Something like =COUNTIFS(AttendanceRange,"Weekly",MaritalStatusRange,"Widowed") will count the surveys that match both conditions,” Layla said. Her chair creaked as she rolled forward an inch, shoulders easing open the way a swimmer loosens before a dive. She let her index finger hover above the F2 key, savoring the phantom click she wouldn't need to make yet. “Okay, that makes sense. So if attendance is in column B and marital status is in column C, would it be =COUNTIFS(B:B,"Weekly",C:C,"Widowed")?” Mika asked. She tilted the monitor back a degree, the spreadsheet’s grid blooming larger across the panel, and her breath stilled while the cells aligned behind her eyes like neatly planted seedlings. The phone beside her elbow pulsed once, but the buzz stayed swallowed under her palm; the vibration rippled out and died unnoticed. “Exactly. That would work if those entries are spelled the same way in every row. If the survey responses use slightly different wording, like “Weekly attendee” or “Widowed spouse,” you'd need to match the exact text or use a wildcard if appropriate,” Layla said. She drew a slow breath that smelled faintly of cold coffee while her thumb traced the ridge of the spacebar, already rehearsing the next refinement. The corner of her lip lifted, then eased back to neutral as the small talk moment passed. “Got it. What if I want to break it down by several attendance groups too, like weekly, monthly, and rarely, all by marital status?” Mika asked. The answer arrived on her tongue the instant he finished; her pulse fluttered against her collarbone the way it might for a dance partner who had signaled the next turn. She clicked once, entering an imagined header row, then flicked her gaze along the formula bar as if watching confetti settle. “Then you’d make separate COUNTIFS formulas for each combination. For example, weekly and widowed, weekly and married, monthly and widowed, and so on. You can also set up a small table and copy the formula across if you keep the criteria references flexible. That’s usually easier than typing each one from scratch,” Layla said. Her fingertips grazed the trackpad again, lingering a half-second longer than necessary to add an unasked-for border to the blank table, the gray stroke sliding into place with a satisfied nod before she released the pad. “That sounds useful. My cousin was asking about something like this too, but I wasn’t sure how to explain it. Is there a way to make the criteria cell-based instead of hardcoding “Weekly” and “Widowed”?” Mika asked. A hush of fluorescent light hummed above while her eyes swam back to the spreadsheet, pupils narrowing slightly at the pleasure of the cell-coordinate puzzle. She inhaled as if tasting the crispness of the range syntax before it left her mouth. “Yes. Put the attendance label in one cell and the marital status in another, then reference those cells in COUNTIFS. For example, =COUNTIFS($B:$B,E2,$C:$C,F2) if E2 has Weekly and F2 has Widowed. That way you can fill the formula down or across for different combinations,” Layla said. The spreadsheet glassed her reflection as she angled the laptop, and her gaze skimmed across the result once more, the cell counts breathing alive in her pupils. A small appreciative breath escaped when the column letters aligned like well-cut keys sliding into a lock. “Nice, that’s exactly what I needed. So I can build a little matrix and just copy the formula through it?” Mika asked. She surfaced from the grid a fraction late, eyelids lifting as if breaking a gentle wave, then rolled her shoulders in a silent stretch of readiness. The overhead light caught the slight curve that brightened her cheeks while the prospect of the matrix took shape in the space between them. “Exactly. Set the row and column labels to the categories you want, then use COUNTIFS with mixed absolute references if needed. If you want, I can help you sketch the layout for the table too,” Layla said.

"Start with the template, make sure the columns are clear, then copy it for each month and keep the structure the same." The smile returned, smaller now but sharp, accompanied by a quick exhalation that might have been a laugh at the neatness of it. "That way the log stays consistent, and you'll be able to scan the counts quickly." The humming stopped. The hands came together, palms rubbed once, and settled.

The morning light had been filtering through the blinds in thin, warm bands, and Valeria had been standing by the window, finishing the last of her coffee, when Sneha's voice cut through the quiet.
